# Transforming a reference into a target

Open this page when the user brings a reference and wants their own version of it: a different person,
object, world, performance, story or audience. Several of these changes often serve a single creative
intention, so think about them together.

Study the supplied material alongside the request and ask: why does the original grab people, and how
could that same pull work for this new person or product? Keep the reference's specific humor, visual
relationships and rhythm in mind while you develop the target. The three documents split the job:

- the reference archive describes the original;
- the Brief records what the user intends;
- the Treatment explains how the new piece will work.

Anything you discover along the way may revise any of the three.

## Preserve the role, then recreate the form

**Keep semantic timing, not seconds.** Recreate the timing intentions of the reference in the target.
If a ranking portrait pops up as each player is introduced and settles when the verdict lands, bind
those two events to the target's own Script. A new speaker or a rewritten argument will change the
seconds; the relationship survives. Keep an explicit clock interval only when that interval is itself
part of the design.

**Find every place a role shows up before you replace it.** A presenter can also be an avatar, a voice,
the subject of B-roll, a face on a phone screen, and the identity inside an MG system. A product can be
handled as a prop, shown on a screen, demonstrated, claimed about, and pitched in the closing CTA. Carry the change through every real
appearance; changing only the most obvious pixels leaves the old identity scattered through the piece.

**Don't copy a form that no longer fits.** Keep the purpose of an action, composition or callback,
and direct a version that suits the new body, the new product, the platform and the viewer. A dog can project
confidence through a squared stance, a steady stare, a tail or the framing, without mimicking a human
hand gesture. A product without a screen can prove the same benefit with a different kind of evidence.

Lenses worth looking through:

| Lens | What to think through |
| --- | --- |
| subject or identity | who carries the narrative role, and every place that identity appears |
| product or brand | what the product truly does and how it is used; its props, UI, logos and colors; the proof, the offer and the CTA |
| Script, language or CTA | meaning, spoken rhythm, words on screen, demonstrations, and every timing relationship that has to track the new wording |
| platform, duration or aspect | which story functions survive, shrink, grow or need a new composition |
| visual or tonal style | a consistent art direction for generated shots, Caption, Typography, MG, Effect and Audio that still keeps the useful structure |
| adding or removing a system | hand the removed system's job to something else instead of leaving a hole in the story |
| several references | state which function comes from which reference and settle conflicts in the Treatment, rather than blending every surface detail |

The lenses are prompts for thinking, not a form to fill in. Follow the relationships this particular
work actually contains.

## Reshape an event from footage

Some adaptations are recognizable by an event unfolding in time rather than by any one subject's look:
a stylized direct-to-camera bit, a dance phrase, a fight, a moving camera, an accident caught by a
locked-off camera. Their appeal may rest on precise timing and physical relationships, which makes them
good places to look for temporal evidence. Work out what carries the appeal: the delivery and the
reaction, the movement, the contact, where the camera sits, the payoff.

The target may change the person, an object, the setting, the words, the sound, the visual world, or
several at once. A person swap is the most common case, and a narrow substitution may leave nearly every
other relationship alone. If the new body, object or story alters what an action
means, rework the action until it serves the target's intention. The Brief and Treatment hold the requested change and your
new creative answer; the source footage remains evidence, not a ready-made target timeline.

Two separate decisions follow:

1. **Observe only.** The footage informs your direction and stays out of the request.
2. **Condition the generation.** A selected excerpt becomes an actual model input, because the target
   must preserve or transfer its temporal behavior.

Take a comic speaking reference: an odd camera angle may combine with a voice that plays a
character, plus matching expression and gesture. Put the intended words in the Script, decide whose voice the target
uses, and direct how the visible performance sells the joke. A supplied portrait settles appearance; it
says nothing about voice or delivery. [Voice direction](../playbooks/craft/voice-direction.md) owns
casting, and
[video direction](../playbooks/craft/video-direction.md#let-footage-carry-temporal-behavior-that-matters)
decides whether, and how, a source excerpt should steer the new shot.

## "Make this with my face"

Say the user hands you a selfie and a football ranking video. The jokes target the players, so the
argument and its comic reveals may fit the new presenter without changes. Put the user into the camera
image carrying the reference's playful swagger, in a framing that works and with space for the board.
Apply the full [image craft](../playbooks/craft/image-direction.md): direct this person's appeal and
presence with the same care you would give any lead performer. One such image can drive every ordinary
talking clip and supply the person's other appearances.

Jokes about the original presenter's own looks or life story need a second look. Decide how their setup
and payoff fit the new casting and the Brief; the new person may suggest a different delivery, or a
better version of the joke. A pet supplied by the user might carry the same confidence through a
planted stance, a stare or deadpan authority. Let the new character make the work feel lived in.

The portrait gives visual identity only. For the voice,
[voice direction](../playbooks/craft/voice-direction.md) works from audio the user supplied or a voice
cast for this performance. The photo's original setting and pose can give way to the shot the video
actually needs. Use [reference relationships](../playbooks/craft/generated-dependencies.md) to decide
where the supplied image enters the production.

## "Use my product"

Say the reference is a podcast recommending a canned drink, and the user wants to promote their
expense-tracking app, whose real benefit is snapping a photo of a receipt. The offhand recommendation,
the doubtful partner and the satisfying demonstration can still carry the exchange. The split-screen
opening, the complementary camera views and speaker-colored Caption may still give it its familiar
shape.

The new conversation might pivot on "You expect me to log every lunch?" A phone demo of photographing
a receipt answers that objection. So the host now has a phone in hand, the cutaway footage shows the
app doing its job, and the graphic reveal reinforces the same moment of understanding. Script, action, imagery and
CTA all grow from what this product genuinely does. Supplied product images and UI fix its appearance;
the user's facts and relevant product material fix its claims. Fill important gaps from the available
material or with the user, while you keep developing the creative idea.

When the person and the product both change, think about them as a pair: why would this person
recommend it, how does the partner react, what makes the encounter fun to watch? The Treatment holds
that answer; the Source carries its words, its references and its semantic links.

## Generation is ordinary production

A useful mental model: the image model is a painter who knows a great deal about the world, the video
model is a similarly knowledgeable camera crew and cast, and audio generation is an equally
knowledgeable sound team. "Real" is an aesthetic you direct (phone capture, natural light, documentary
handheld, room tone), not a statement about where the asset came from. Making the target media is the
normal task.

External references become essential when general world knowledge cannot pin down an exact identity:
the user themselves, a private or niche product, a proprietary interface, an exact logo or data
display. Once a generated image, video or sound is accepted, it can serve as the authority for later
work.

Match the generation relationship to the control you need:

| Control | When |
| --- | --- |
| reference-conditioned video | identity, art direction, composition or product continuity matters; the input steers the shot without having to be its literal first or last frame |
| keyframe-constrained video | the endpoint image is itself part of the designed continuous change |
| text-only video | the shot gains from variety and has no exact visual anchor to honor |

These describe what the control means. Whether a given package or Provider supports it is stated in
that package's public documentation.

## Keep media dependencies shallow and useful

Generate pictures the work will actually use, such as the principal A-roll camera image, its reverse
angle, a B-roll still, a product close-up or the content of a component slot. The first accepted presenter image is often already the
main A-roll camera image. You can derive a further A-roll view from it by describing the new camera
relationship, the eye line, recurring objects, the light, and what naturally comes into view when the
camera faces the other way. The shared world exists in how the useful shots relate, not in an abstract
background plate.

Make a standalone identity image or location image only when the work itself needs one. Otherwise,
avoid chains of in-between pictures whose only purpose is to feed the next generation. When a single
shot is split because of a model's duration limit, reuse the same accepted visual authorities for every
part, unless the cut is deliberately designed around a literal boundary frame.

Bring accepted media into the composition through Source and Run Candidates, then build Caption, MG,
B-roll coverage, effects and sound around them, judging in Studio and rendered Results. When a generated
item fails at its job, fix the responsible prompt, reference relationship or shot design before
deciding on another authorized request. Revisit the Treatment when the creative answer itself changes.
