# Short drama

Short drama is storytelling led by performance. The characters do more than deliver information:
what they want, how they react, what they do, how they relate and where they are is what makes one
event cause the next. This is rich A-roll. Kitchen arguments, staged meetings, podcast exchanges and
street interviews can all take on this dramatic shape whenever a human event carries the story.

## Every passage has a dramatic job

Direct from the causal movement in the Treatment. Typical engines: someone wants something; someone
else changes what it means; an action has a consequence; a reveal changes what the viewer understands.
Any of these can carry it: lines, silence, a reaction, a bit of physical business.

**A Segment is a performable dramatic passage.** It is not a mandatory camera shot or speaker turn. One
Segment can hold several people and several cuts; equally, when a passage works better built in pieces,
several generated clips can return to the same camera views. Pick whichever shape keeps the intended
action and exchange readable.

**Time.** Estimate the Script to learn its speaking density, then add whatever time the planned pause,
reaction and action require. The selected video model's supported request lengths limit what can be
generated together, but they do not define the story's scenes.
[Script and time](../../creation/script-and-time.md#estimate-before-choosing-durations) owns
estimation, and
[generated video direction](../craft/video-direction.md#size-the-request-around-the-delivery) owns how
the model's request range shapes the performable passage.

## Set up the views the story needs

When the work rests on recognizable people, a shared world, staging or composition,
reference-conditioned generation is a strong place to start. A key image can establish a character, a
whole story view with the characters in their setting, or both. Direct the images and the video
passages in natural language for the dramatic intention at hand. Because dramatic scenes vary so much,
free direction usually serves them best; a reusable Prompt Kit pays off only once repeated productions reveal a stable prompt
relationship worth keeping.

Your opening image may be a reference for one character, or a picture of the encounter as a whole. Derive later
views from whichever images already hold the facts they need:

- a character image lends identity to a view that sets up a new location and a new dramatic situation;
- a shared two-person view fixes the bodies, the line of gaze and the encounter before any closer views;
- one story view passes a costume, a prop or a spatial relationship on to the next;
- an image the user supplied may go straight in if it already shows the right character in the right shot.

In a single location, a few well-chosen views can feed a large number of generated clips. For a
conversation on a sofa, one view per side of the conversation may hold everything the passage needs, with modest
reframing, performance and camera changes happening in the video itself. Another image earns its place
when a new view has to establish an important fact: a changed relationship, a decisive prop state, a
part of the space the plot depends on.

**Two separate decisions:** which views to establish as images, and which dramatic action to generate
together as one clip. A single generated clip can move through several shots or places while key images
pin down the people, objects, composition or continuity that need explicit visual direction; a single
view can also guide several clips. A new place deserves an image when the story needs that specific
view established; other moves and cuts can develop inside the video passage. The image graph follows
visual dependencies, not the number of locations or edits.

The edges between images are the business of
[reference relationships](../craft/generated-dependencies.md). For two contrasting ways to set up a
conversational world, compare the [two-person podcast](two-person-podcast.md) and
[street interview](street-interview.md) pages.
[Video direction](../craft/video-direction.md#direct-camera-and-cuts-as-part-of-the-passage) owns the
shots inside a generated passage and its reference relationship.

## One world, a different slice per camera

The background tells story. It can signal intimacy or status, explain why these people are together,
create a comic mismatch, plant an object the plot will use, or let a new scene read at once. Direct its mood
with the same conviction you give the characters; don't leave the model to fill in generic space.

Related views share whatever makes the place recognizable: atmosphere, materials, palette, social
setting, spatial layout. Each camera also sees its own believable slice of that place. One camera can
show the part of the room around one character while another shows a different sector around their
partner. How person, setting and view relate is covered in
[image direction](../craft/image-direction.md#establish-the-place-with-a-few-details).

## The performance carries the event

Action direction should preserve cause and attention: who starts it, who notices, what changes hands,
what a reaction is answering, and why attention or the camera moves at this moment. Describe the
behavior and the views this passage needs. A glance, an interruption, a withheld answer or turning away
matters when it shifts the dramatic relationship.

Other layers (Caption, Typography, MG, effects, B-roll) may sharpen the event, but the human spine
stays. An
on-screen text message can disclose a fact, an insert can confirm what a character is looking at, and a
graphic can make a payoff legible. Keep the A-roll performance and its sound meaningful even while another layer covers
the picture.

When reviewing the finished piece, check dramatic causality and the coherence of people, places and
action: at every point the viewer should understand who drives the action, what has shifted, and
why the next view or scene comes next. A new short-drama form may arrange these relationships differently, but it keeps that
responsibility.
