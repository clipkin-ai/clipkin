# Directing graphic composition

Use this page for boards, comparisons, charts, titles, cards, diagrams and any designed visual field.
Begin with what the viewer should grasp or feel. A graphic may explain, organize, set a mood, land a joke
or simply delight; it does not need to justify itself as literal information. How the picture changes over
time belongs to [Motion graphics](motion-graphics.md).

## Compose an idea, not a stack of decorations

Settle three things first: what the eye lands on first, what backs it up, and what changes at the next
meaningful beat. Express that order through scale, spacing, grouping, contrast, color and motion. Structure
alone can speak before anyone reads a word: the rows of a ranking, an interview's empty answer slots, the
two halves of a product comparison.

Design the whole frame as one thing:

- A presenter's position can open space for a board.
- One accent color can link Caption, an answer icon, a question mark and a reveal flash.
- Palette, occupied area and brightness interact. A big pale panel behind pale clothing can flatten the
  shot even if each piece looks good alone.

Work from the real material's framing and motion; the next section covers how source direction and
authored presentation answer each other.

Pin the visual direction down before rolling it out across scenes. Reduce a reference's vibe to a handful
of shared choices: palette, type, edges, depth and motion character. A short representative passage can
show them working together on the actual footage. Once accepted, move those choices into shared Recipes
or project styling so that a later change propagates consistently to every related component.

Give graphic text a job Caption does not already do: identifying, comparing or emphasizing what the
picture needs. If you delete a redundant heading or label, recompose what remains and its spacing. The old
text slot should not linger as an unexplained gap; the demonstration must still feel finished.

## Give material and graphics room together

Hold three relationships apart:

| Layer | What it organizes |
| --- | --- |
| The generated or recorded scene | People interacting with the people, props and setting around them |
| The camera view | How that scene is framed |
| The final Canvas | Authored media views, Caption and graphics arranged into the delivered picture |

A sign recorded in the scene belongs to the scene; an explanatory label belongs to a component.

When directing new footage, plan room for the graphics that will recur. Express that need as camera facts:
where the subject sits, how much of the body is visible, room for gestures, distance and background. Give
the image model those picture requirements only; the future MG, editorial text and explanation stay with
their own components. [Image direction](image-direction.md#frame-the-image-for-what-it-will-become) owns
the wording. Once footage is accepted, its framing is a concrete fact to work with: change how the footage is
viewed, change the graphic, or rearrange both, according to what must remain legible.

Decide which contribution leads at each moment. A small moving face can hold attention while a large calm
field supports it. Protect whatever carries the current beat: the mouth, a meaningful gesture, a product
fact or a line of text. Placement, source crop, occupied extent and paint order all shape the result, and
[Spatial layout](../../production/spatial.md) owns their geometry. Being A-roll gives material no reserved
region and no permanent place at the back of the stack.

When a screen carries the claim, [screen demonstrations](screen-demonstrations.md) helps you decide whether
to show captured proof or build an authored explanation. That view still shares the Canvas geometry and the visual
hierarchy of the composition around it.

## Put the right facts in editable structure

Decide what is built and what is placed:

| Build it as authored structure (text, exact fonts, geometry, state) when... | Use media (supplied, found or generated) when... |
| --- | --- |
| wording, alignment, timing, data or repeated visual relationships must be controlled | the picture itself is the content: photographs, artwork, textures, product imagery |

A real screenshot or a finished graphic can stay media; rebuilding every pixel as a component is not
automatically worthwhile.

Where the line falls depends on the work. Signage caught on camera is part of the scene it was filmed in. A
score that has to change when someone answers is editable graphic state. A textured paper background could be drawn, generated
procedurally, found or produced by an image model; pick by the look you want, theming and how much it must
be edited later.

An icon, portrait, interface or other picture might identify who or what we are talking about, set a
theme, document a real state or be the punchline. What the viewer needs from it decides where it should come from:

- **The asset is itself evidence** (exact current wording, data, UI, a private identity, a particular brand
  asset): take it from the authoritative supplied or found source, and note in the project where it
  came from and any conditions on its use.
- **The job is recognition or creative adaptation** of a familiar public icon, person, object, meme or
  place: a broadly informed image model can paint the material directly or from references, and a found
  asset is an equally ordinary option. Check that what comes back is recognizably the intended subject and
  does its graphic job. Reconstruction keeps the useful recognizability and relationship; adaptation may swap them for a
  new theme.

Where an image came from does not by itself make it more visually truthful than another. Generation prompts
and reference edges live in Source, so the creative intent behind them remains open to editing.

## Carry the picture into the component

Set component boundaries by behavior. A ranking board whose rows move and keep state together can have one
visual owner; a label and an unrelated photo can stay peers. Size and beauty of the graphic do not decide
it. [Component design](../../production/component-design.md) covers where the boundary goes and which inputs
and controls it exposes. What the viewer experiences as change is the subject of
[Motion graphics](motion-graphics.md), and
[review](../../production/review.md#make-the-composition-work) checks how that change reads against the
real material.
